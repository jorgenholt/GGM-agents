\section{Dividing the work between people, code and agents}

The previous sections describe what agents have been asked to do.
This section turns to a question every one of those systems answers, explicitly or not: which parts of the work go to the language model, which to ordinary code, and which stay with a person.
The question is older than LLMs, and the older answers frame the recent evidence.

\subsection{Earlier answers: human factors and the economics of tasks}

Research on human interaction with automation has asked for four decades which functions to automate and to what degree.
\citet{parasuraman2000model} distinguish four functions, from acquiring and analysing information to choosing and carrying out an action, and argue that each can be automated to a different level, chosen by weighing the consequences for human performance, the reliability of the automation and the cost of its errors.
\citet{bainbridge1983ironies} pointed out the central irony: automating the routine parts of a task leaves people with the hardest parts, while giving them less practice at the skills those parts require.
Later work found that people over-rely on automated advice, and that this automation bias affects experts as well as novices and is not removed by training \citep{parasuraman2010complacency}.
For this thesis, the implication is that placing a person at a decision point is not enough on its own; the person must also be able and willing to disagree with the agent.

Economics approaches the same question through tasks rather than functions.
In the task-based view, technology substitutes for workers in tasks that follow explicit rules and complements them in non-routine problem-solving \citep{autor2003skill}, while automation also creates new tasks in which people have the advantage \citep{acemoglu2019automation}.
Applied to LLMs, the approach rates each task in an occupation for exposure, distinguishing what a language model can do alone from what it can do with additional software \citep{eloundou2024gpts}.
A field experiment with management consultants shows why the rating has to be made task by task: AI assistance improved performance on tasks within its capabilities but worsened it on a task just outside them that looked similar \citep{dellacqua2026navigating}.
Chapter 5 applies the same task-by-task logic to a single modelling workflow.

\subsection{From full autonomy to gated pipelines}

Recent work on AI in science spans a range of positions on this question.
At one end are systems built for full autonomy.
The AI Scientist generates research ideas, writes and runs experiments, and drafts and reviews complete machine-learning papers with little human involvement, at a reported cost of under 15 dollars per paper \citep{lu2024ai}.
Coscientist plans and executes chemistry experiments through laboratory automation \citep{boiko2023autonomous}, ChemCrow gives a language model eighteen expert-designed chemistry tools \citep{bran2024augmenting}, and Co-Scientist generates and ranks biomedical hypotheses for experimental testing \citep{gottweis2026accelerating}.
Surveys of these scientific agents identify domain tools and validation mechanisms as what separates them from general-purpose assistants \citep{ren2025towards}.

Evaluations temper these claims.
ScienceAgentBench draws 102 tasks from 44 peer-reviewed publications and asks agents to write the analysis code for each; the best agent solved about a third of them \citep{chen2025scienceagentbench}.
Its authors conclude that agents should be assessed on the individual tasks of a scientific workflow before claims are made about automating the workflow end to end, a conclusion this thesis adopts.

In economics, \citet{korinek2023generative} catalogues dozens of uses of LLMs across ideation, writing, background research, coding, data analysis and mathematical derivation, and argues that the main near-term gains come from automating many small tasks within the researcher's own workflow.
A later guide gives economists working code for agents that review literature, write econometric code and retrieve data \citep{korinek2025ai}.
Both leave the researcher to decide what is worth doing and whether the result holds.

\subsection{Experimental evidence that the division matters}

The most direct evidence on how to divide the work comes from a group of three papers on human-in-the-loop economic research by the same authors.
HLER is a multi-agent pipeline for empirical economics that audits a dataset, generates hypotheses constrained by what the data can support, runs the econometric analysis and drafts a paper, with human decisions at fixed points \citep{zhu2026hler}.
\citet{zhu2026human} then test whether its reliability comes from the model or from how the work is organised.
In a pre-specified experiment with 280 complete research runs on four datasets, an unconstrained multi-agent pipeline produced a critical failure in 72\% of runs.
With the same model, agents and prompts, the failure rate fell to 16\% when three rules were imposed: the LLM reasons but does not do the data work, data preparation and estimation are done by deterministic code, and a person decides at three gates, namely the choice of research question, the identification strategy and the decision to publish.
An ablation suggests that deterministic computation and human gates each reduce failures on their own, and reduce them most when combined.
The most common failures in the unconstrained pipeline were hallucinated references, inconsistent interpretation, infeasible research questions and weak identification, not errors in executing code.
The authors note the limits: one model, four datasets, and an ablation with only twenty runs per condition.
It should be added that the system tested is the authors' own, evaluated within their own study design.

In pAI-Econ-claude, the same authors turn to economic theory, a harder case \citep{zhu2026pai}.
They start from the observation that many outputs in economics have no cheap, complete, machine-readable signal of whether they are correct: a proof sketch, a mechanism or a welfare claim cannot be checked the way code can be tested.
Their gates therefore \emph{diagnose} specific failures and recommend going back a step, but explicitly do not certify that what passes is correct.
Decisions that are costly to reverse, such as the choice of equilibrium concept, are reserved for the researcher.
In five paired comparisons, two blinded evaluators preferred the gated workflow in four, though it used several times the resources of the ungated baseline.

\subsection{Summary}

The recent evidence and the older literature point the same way.
The language model should reason and propose, deterministic code should compute, and people should decide where decisions are hard to undo.
The engineering systems in Sections 2.2 to 2.5 arrive at the same division independently, through harnesses, validators and solvers that produce every number.
The older literature adds two warnings: a human gate only helps if the person at it is able and inclined to disagree, and automating the routine work can erode the expertise the gate depends on.
The AI-for-economics literature adds a third point, that some outputs cannot be scored against a right answer.
Where that is so, automated checks can find specific faults, but deciding that a result is good enough remains a human judgment.
