\section{Calibrating existing models}

Calibration is the task closest to the core of this thesis.
Automating it with optimisation algorithms is decades old \citep{duan1992effective}, and so is the finding that a good fit does not settle a calibration: different parameter sets can often reproduce the observations equally well, a property known in hydrology as \emph{equifinality} \citep{beven2006manifesto}.
What is new is giving the task to a language model.

Among calibration studies, the one closest to GGM's problem uses an LLM as the optimiser for a disease simulation model with ten parameters \citep{gomezguillen2026agentic}.
At each step the model receives the parameters with their meanings and bounds, constraints written in plain language, and the history of earlier guesses with their errors broken down by age group; it returns a new parameter vector and a written rationale.
A harness runs every simulation and rejects infeasible proposals, so the LLM never touches the model directly.
The authors call this a \emph{grey-box} problem: the parameters are interpretable, but their joint effect is hard to predict, which is where an expert reasons directionally from the pattern of errors.
Without constraints, the LLM reached a better fit than Bayesian optimisation with 16 model runs instead of 110, but took four to seven times as long, and its results are reproducible only with a fixed model and deterministic settings.
The authors argue that the written rationales make the search auditable, but do not test whether people find them useful.

How close such agents come to experts is shown most clearly in hydrology.
\citet{li2026hydroagent} benchmark nine frontier LLMs on calibrating a flood-forecasting model used by the US National Weather Service.
Only one reached the human expert's result, and only at one of four gauges.
The recurring failures were stopping too early, proposing physically implausible values, and diagnosing a problem correctly but then adjusting the wrong parameters.
A peer-reviewed comparison with established optimisers reaches a mixed verdict: one of five LLMs outperformed them, and the other four did worse \citep{zhu2026large}.

Other studies give the LLM a narrower role.
In PetroGraph, agents organise the history matching of oil reservoirs while a conventional optimiser does the search \citep{samigullin2026multi}.
The mismatch fell by 95\% on a small synthetic reservoir but by only 13\% on a real field, and the human checkpoints in its design were skipped in every reported experiment.
Data2BEM similarly lets GPT-4-based agents build a building energy model and configure its calibration, which a genetic algorithm then performs \citep{lu2025automated}.

Two points emerge.
LLMs can take part in calibration, but the best results fall short of skilled experts on real models, and what is gained in model runs is lost in time and reproducibility.
And in every study the targets are observations and the parameters are physical or epidemiological quantities, whose plausibility can be checked against measurements.
Section 2.6 returns to why this is not the case for a market equilibrium model such as GGM.
